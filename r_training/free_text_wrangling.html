About this session

This is a stand-alone introduction to free text wrangling in R using the tidytext package. It’s heavily based on Julia Silge and David Robinson’s book about text mining, which is an excellent resource if you’re looking for applications and extensions to this session.

The aim of the session is to explore some tools and techniques for wrangling free text: basically, ways of taking wild-caught bits of text, and tidying them up so that they can be analysed. Note that we won’t really talk about that analysis itself - and for details of what you can do with your tidied text, you can find lots of examples in Silge and Robinson 2025.

We also don’t get into the non-tidy formats used in some kinds of text analysis. Again, Silge and Robinson has a lot of helpful guidance for e.g. DTM and the like.

You might also find our session on the stringr package handy as a primer on a modern toolkit for working with strings in R. You’ll also want some decent dplyr skills too - text wrangling gets complicated very quickly!

TipSession resources
  • you don’t need anything apart from an R setup for this session with a few packages installed (see the setup chunk below)
  • you’ll almost certainly want the stringr cheatsheet on hand

What’s the problem?

This chunk of text is hard to analyse! For example, there isn’t a simple way of finding even how many words we’re working with. Okay, so we can count characters using nchar() or similar, but we can’t do much else with the text. In this session, we’ll introduce the tidytext approach to working with text data. That’s tidyverse-allied, so it’ll come as no shock at all that we’re going to start my turning our text into a simple tibble:

ww <- tibble(ww)

Sentiments

It’s important to note that we lose a lot of meaning when we break sentences into individual words. That said, looking at individual words can tell you about your topic. One example is by using a sentiment lexicon: a group of words that have been scored for their connotations. For example:

get_sentiments("bing") 
# A tibble: 6,786 × 2
   word        sentiment
   <chr>       <chr>

 2 abnormal    negative

We could join those too:

ww |>
  unnest_tokens(word, ww) |>
  anti_join(get_stopwords()) |>
  inner_join(get_sentiments("bing")) |> # this will discard any of the non-sentiment words
  count(sentiment)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  sentiment     n
  <chr>     <int>
1 negative      9
2 positive      8

That’s perhaps a surprise - I’d have thought the darkly portentous start of the War of the Worlds would be a bit more negative. What about Sherlock?

Frequencies

We’ve already calculated simple counts of word occurrence from our text. If you’ve got several documents - like our tibble of sentences - you can also calculate frequencies and more using bind_tf_idf():

ww_sent |>
  unnest_tokens(word, sentence) |>
  anti_join(get_stopwords()) |>
  count(word, sentence_no, sort = T) |>
  bind_tf_idf(word, sentence_no, n) |>
  arrange(desc(tf_idf))
# A tibble: 108 × 6

  • tf = term frequency, how common the word is within the document (in this case, our sentence)
  • idf = inverse document frequency, how uncommon a word is in the whole corpus (group of sentences). Larger = rarer
  • tf_idf = tf * idf, basically correlating with overall-rare words at unusually high frequency in a document
books <- c("A Scandal in Bohemia", "The Red-Headed League", "A Case of Identity", "The Boscombe Valley Mystery", "The Five Orange Pips", "The Man with the Twisted Lip", "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle", "The Adventure of the Speckled Band", "The Adventure of the Engineer’s Thumb", "The Adventure of the Noble Bachelor", "The Adventure of the Beryl Coronet", "The Adventure of the Copper Beeches") 

books_col <- books |>
  toupper() |>
  paste0(collapse="|")

tibble(raw = readr::read_lines(here::here("r_training/data/pg1661.txt"))) |>
  mutate(book = cumsum(str_detect(raw, books_col))) |>
  filter(book > 0) |>
  mutate(book = (\(x) books[x])(book)) |>
  unnest_tokens(word, raw) |>
  filter(str_detect(word, "_", negate = T)) |> # lot of non-english words with _s in them
  filter(str_detect(word, "\\d", negate = T)) |> # and a surprising number of numbers, which aren't removed
  anti_join(get_stopwords()) |>
  count(book, word) |>
  distinct() |>
  bind_tf_idf(book, word, n) |>
  arrange(desc(tf_idf)) |>
  group_by(book) |>
  slice_max(tf_idf, n = 3, with_ties = F) # I think most of these words are unique to the book where they appear
